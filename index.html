Project R26-IT-047 Smart Disaster Relief and Donation Coordination System

Decision support for flood and landslide response,
built for transparent assistance.

During severe monsoon floods and landslides in Sri Lanka, responders face acute information scarcity, damaged infrastructure, and overwhelming casualty queues. SmartRelief links emergency SOS reporting, historical hazard prediction, computer-vision relief demand estimation, clinical triage decision support, and multilingual donation appeal evaluation into an integrated decision-support system where every automated output is strictly advisory and open to human override.

Research Architecture

The Three Core Components

Investigating modular machine-learning pipelines across emergency alerting, relief prediction, and clinical triage.

Component 1 · Owner: Kavitha

Emergency Alerting, Camp Planning and Hazard Prediction

SOS Alerting & Priority: Victim intake form with GPS coordinates, validated payload, and Random Forest regressor priority scoring with status tracking (Active, Acknowledged, Assigned, Responding, Resolved) alerting authorised responders via FastAPI with JWT role checks.

Camp Recommendation: Ranks candidate temporary medical camp sites on a Leaflet map using hazard risk, SOS demand, roads, population, and facilities. High suitability never overrides hard safety constraints; decisions require authority review.

Disaster Prediction: Historical flood (Logistic Regression, 82.4%) and landslide (Random Forest, 91.3%) risk maps from rainfall, slope, and land-cover data . Historical models are explicitly not live forecasts.

Component 2 · Owner: Rashmi

Integrated Disaster Relief Prediction

Affected-Population Head Counting: YOLOv8n detector trained on Roboflow Universe Head Detection dataset v1 won controlled evaluation against YOLO11n and YOLO26n on 422 validation images / 4,449 heads (precision 0.914, recall 0.798, mAP50 0.886).

Demand & Forecast: Essential relief needs calculated using official per-person and per-family rates. Next-hour camp population forecasted via Random Forest time-series regression exported as lightweight JSON decision trees for in-browser execution.

Donation Prioritisation: Random Forest scoring donor contribution probability, geographic proximity prioritisation, and dynamic allocation retiring fulfilled items while maintaining focus on critical shortages.

Component 3 · Owner: D.K.S.D. Perera

Patient Severity Triage and Appeal Quality Scoring

Clinical Triage: Intake form with symptoms, age, 6 vitals, scispaCy specialty routing (17 specialties, 10 condition groups, Pediatric/Geriatric aware), 31 critical triggers bypassing the model, and a 5-model stacked ensemble with raise-only safety layers (n=740 test: balanced accuracy 0.885, HIGH recall 0.905, 5 of 211 HIGH assigned LOW). Outputs are advisory with Medical Officer override.

Multilingual Appeal Scoring: Rates English, Sinhala, and Tamil relief appeals (LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH) using TF-IDF, LSA, calibrated Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting score regressor without pretrained embeddings (n=1,126 test: accuracy 0.804, MAE 0.333, R² 0.755).

Literature Survey

↑ Back to Domain TOC

Disaster medical triage

Triage allocates scarce clinical attention by urgency, and its errors are asymmetric: ranking a patient who needs urgent care too low may cost a life, whereas ranking one too high only spends limited attention. Systematic reviews report under-triage of critically ill patients [1], substantial variation in triage-system performance across settings [2], and persistent under-triage across all five-level systems [3], with performance especially variable where resources are limited [4]. The Emergency Severity Index (ESI) is the most studied five-level instrument [12]. Emergency-department, prehospital and disaster triage differ in population and case mix, so results do not transfer directly between them.

Machine learning for triage

Machine-learning triage has been reported to separate patient outcomes better than the ESI [7], and chief-complaint text improves outcome and disposition prediction [8], [9]. Stacked generalisation [13] over gradient-boosted trees such as LightGBM [14] and XGBoost [15] is a strong baseline for tabular clinical data. However, a systematic review of artificial intelligence in emergency and disaster triage finds little validation in genuine disaster settings [10]. A further methodological hazard is data leakage: repeated records across training and test splits inflate reported performance [11], and when labels are constructed from the input features, accuracy measures how well a rule is recovered rather than how well clinical outcomes are predicted.

Donation appeals and crowdfunding text

Message concreteness shapes donation intentions [5], and appeals that combine information quality with gain framing increase willingness to donate [6]. Large language models can write appeals that outperform human-written ones on engagement [16]. For scoring such text, latent semantic analysis [17], Random Forests [18], gradient boosting [19] and sigmoid or isotonic calibration [20] form an auditable pipeline, although models can exploit spurious cues [21], which motivates shortcut baselines. Appeal-quality analysis is almost entirely English, and large crowdfunding datasets such as MDCC [24] cover general campaigns rather than disaster relief. In Sri Lanka, Donate.lk [22] routed donations but did not assess appeal text.

Hazard prediction, crowd estimation and relief logistics

Literature for flood and landslide prediction, crowd counting and relief-demand estimation is being compiled by the respective component owners and will be added here.

Research Gap

↑ Back to Domain TOC

Four gaps motivate this work.

(1) Manual relief estimation. In existing disaster management practice, essential relief needs are largely estimated manually, which delays distribution and leads to inefficient donation coordination. Automated estimates based on observed affected populations and official requirement rates are not routinely available.

(2) Unvalidated disaster triage. Triage decision support is well studied in emergency departments but has little validation in disaster settings [10]; reported scores are frequently inflated by repeated records across splits [11] or by labels that restate the inputs.

(3) Language coverage of appeal assessment. Appeal-quality research is almost entirely English [5], [6], and no public disaster-relief appeal corpus with quality labels in Sinhala or Tamil is known to the authors.

(4) Separate tools. Existing Sri Lankan donation tooling such as Donate.lk [22] routes donations but does not assess appeal text, and hazard prediction, emergency reporting, camp planning, triage and donor coordination are rarely connected in one workflow.

Research Problem

↑ Back to Domain TOC

During floods and landslides in Sri Lanka, responders must decide where hazard risk is highest, who is affected and where they shelter, which patients need care first, and which supplies to request from whom, often with incomplete data, few clinicians and unreliable connectivity. Current practice relies on manual estimation and unaided judgement, so relief is delayed and donations are poorly targeted. The research problem is: how can a coordinated set of machine-learning components estimate hazard risk, affected population, relief requirements, patient severity and appeal quality accurately enough, and transparently enough, to support (not replace) human decision-makers in flood and landslide response, and how should such components be evaluated honestly given scarce disaster-specific data?

Research Objectives

↑ Back to Domain TOC

Main objective

To design, implement and evaluate an integrated, decision-support platform for flood and landslide relief in Sri Lanka that links emergency reporting, hazard prediction, population and relief-demand estimation, medical triage and donation coordination, with every automated output advisory and open to human override.

Component 1 - Emergency Alerting, Camp Planning and Hazard Prediction

  • Capture GPS-tagged SOS requests securely and assign a priority score for responders.
  • Predict flood and landslide risk for supported areas from historical records and environmental features, and present it as a map layer.
  • Rank candidate temporary medical camp sites from hazard risk, SOS demand, accessibility and nearby facilities, subject to hard safety constraints and authority approval.

Component 2 - Integrated Disaster Relief Prediction

  • Estimate the affected population at a safety centre from images using a head-detection model selected by controlled comparison of YOLOv8n, YOLO11n and YOLO26n.
  • Convert population estimates and official per-person and per-family rates into structured donation requests.
  • Forecast next-hour relief-camp occupancy from camp, rainfall and water-level time series, deployable offline in the browser.
  • Prioritise donors by predicted contribution probability and proximity, and adapt requests as donations arrive.

Component 3 - Patient Severity Triage and Multilingual Appeal Quality Scoring

  • Rank patients at disaster medical camps by severity with a recall-oriented design in which HIGH-to-LOW errors are minimised, rule-based critical triggers bypass the model, and a Medical Officer can override.
  • Route patients to the appropriate specialty using clinical entity extraction and age-aware rules.
  • Score English, Sinhala and Tamil donation appeals as LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH quality with a numeric score, using no pretrained embeddings and with the corpus composition disclosed.
  • Evaluate both under leakage-aware protocols and state the validation each still requires.

Methodology

↑ Back to Domain TOC

Each component follows an offline training and application-time inference split: data are collected and cleaned, features engineered, candidate models compared and a model selected offline, then the saved model and its preprocessing run at application time behind a FastAPI service and a React interface.

Component 1 Methodology — Emergency Alerting, Camp Planning and Hazard Prediction

Data: historical flood and landslide records, with rainfall, SRTM elevation and derived slope, ESA land cover and SMAP soil moisture where used, aligned to geographic boundaries . Data preparation aligns coordinate reference systems and resolution, extracts features per geographic unit, associates them with historical labels, cleans missing values, and splits data while avoiding leakage between neighbouring locations. Models: Logistic Regression and Random Forest were compared for flood prediction, with Logistic Regression reported as selected (82.4%), and Random Forest for landslides (91.3%); metric names and test splits are to be confirmed . SOS priority uses a Random Forest regressor over urgency, number of affected people, vulnerable people and risk; camp suitability uses a Random Forest regressor over affected population, SOS demand, accessibility, nearby services and hazard exposure, followed by hard safety and operational constraints. The SOS endpoint validates a JWT, checks role, validates the payload, scores priority, stores the request and notifies authorised responders. Camp candidates are shown on a Leaflet map for authority review. A prediction is not a guarantee that a hazard will occur, and live prediction would additionally require an ingestion source, refresh schedule and stale-data handling.

Component 2 Methodology — Integrated Disaster Relief Prediction

Population estimation: the Roboflow Universe Head Detection dataset (v1) provides head images with bounding boxes. Three candidate detectors (YOLOv8n, YOLO11n, YOLO26n) were trained from scratch under identical conditions with patience-based early stopping and a 150-epoch maximum, and compared on a held-out validation set of 422 images containing 4,449 heads. YOLOv8n gave the best overall result and was selected; counts from several images of one centre are summed to cover large sites. Relief demand: the estimated count is multiplied by government-defined per-person and per-family requirement rates to give item quantities, which become structured donation requests. Forecasting: hourly camp population is modelled as a time-series regression with lagged population, short-term change, rolling mean and cumulative rainfall features, using Random Forest regression, exported as JSON trees for in-browser inference. Donor targeting: a Random Forest regression scores donor contribution probability from history, item categories, quantities, response time and distance, combined with geographic proximity; dynamic allocation tracks donated versus remaining quantities and retires fulfilled items.

Component 3 Methodology — Patient Severity Triage and Multilingual Appeal Quality Scoring

Triage data and labels: 7,578 NHAMCS 2022 emergency-department visits [23], relabelled LOW (3,124), MEDIUM (2,472) or HIGH (1,982) under a clinician-guided policy built on vital-sign thresholds, red-flag patterns and procedure codes and reviewed by a medical professional. Because the policy uses vitals and red flags that are also inputs, the task is policy reproduction, and this is stated as a limitation. Splits: 80/10/10 by stratified group sampling on patient profile (identical complaint text and age), giving train 6,090, validation 748 and test 740, so no profile crosses splits; preprocessing is fitted on training data only. Features: 1,457 linear and 193 tree features including age bands, six vitals with missing indicators, eleven vital-threshold flags, six red flags, and 1-2-gram TF-IDF of the complaint. Models: three logistic regressions, LightGBM and XGBoost as base models; five-fold profile-grouped out-of-fold probabilities feed a logistic-regression meta-learner. Decision policy: a threshold cascade selected by grid search to maximise balanced recall subject to HIGH recall of at least 0.90, with two raise-only safety layers; a rule engine of 31 critical triggers bypasses the model. Evaluation: bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, Wilson intervals, under- versus over-triage accounting, robustness tests (missing vitals, noise, typed free text, subgroups, resampled prevalence) and a safety-layer ablation.

Appeal data: 7,100 collected appeals (English 4,600; Sinhala 1,250; Tamil 1,250) with quality scores 1-5 mapped to LOW (1-2), MEDIUM (3) and HIGH (4-5). English text comes from a subset of MDCC [24] (CC BY-NC 4.0), a public GoFundMe research corpus and other public sources; Sinhala and Tamil text is mostly native-reviewed augmentation, with a small web-sourced portion and some AI-generated boundary cases. To limit augmented share, 40% of augmented or AI-generated Sinhala and Tamil rows are kept, giving 5,627 appeals (4,600 English, 511 Sinhala, 516 Tamil). Pipeline: per-language branches (word 1-2-gram TF-IDF with 150-dimensional LSA for English; character 3-5-gram TF-IDF with 50-dimensional LSA for Sinhala and Tamil), thirteen handcrafted features, a calibrated Random Forest classifier and a Gradient Boosting regressor with isotonic correction. Evaluation: stratified 20% hold-out (1,126 appeals) plus 5-fold cross-validation; accuracy, macro-F1, per-class recall, MAE and R².

Limitations and Validation Status

↑ Back to Domain TOC
  • All three components are working prototypes; the evidence supports the engineering and the evaluation, not clinical or donor-behaviour claims.
  • Triage: both the labels and features derive from vitals, red flags and complaint patterns, so scores measure policy reproduction. NHAMCS is United States emergency-department data and differs from disaster-camp populations. Five HIGH-to-LOW errors in 211 HIGH patients (95% CI 1.0-5.4%) mean under-triage is reduced but not eliminated; 41 MEDIUM patients were promoted to HIGH. HIGH recall is lowest for patients aged 65 or over (0.765, 51 HIGH patients). Typed free-text complaints that do not match the coded complaints cost balanced accuracy (0.885 to 0.802) before the complaint mapper restores it. Needed: independent outcome labels on camp-like data, calibration analysis, and an ethics-approved pilot.
  • Appeals: Sinhala and Tamil accuracy (0.88 and 0.90) largely reflects augmented or AI-generated text and should not be read as quality prediction; only 20 Sinhala and 28 Tamil appeals are web-sourced. The random test split may allow near-duplicates on both sides. Needed: blinded annotation of real Sinhala and Tamil appeals by two native speakers, with augmented text kept out of the test set. Appeal scores advise coordinators and must not decide whether a request is genuine; appeal text drawn from real campaigns must be paraphrased or anonymised, and the MDCC licence (attribution, non-commercial) applies.
  • Hazard prediction: a historical model is not a live forecast, and a high camp-suitability score never replaces reliable hazard data and human review.
  • Before field use the platform needs access control, rate limiting, consent and a retention policy.
